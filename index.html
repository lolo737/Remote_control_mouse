The threat model

A mouse is the best place to hide.

It sits closer to you than your phone, it never once asks for permission, and it is already trusted to track every movement you make. Turning one into a surveillance implant is not a film plot — it is a supply-chain decision taken four steps upstream of your desk, by someone you will never meet.

  1. 01

    The swap

    A receiver arrives in a plain envelope. Or a firmware update is signed by someone who is not the vendor. Nothing on the desk looks any different, and that is the entire point.

  2. 02

    The senses

    Pointer motion, click rhythm, the pause before a password — and, if a microphone fits the shell, the room itself.

  3. 03

    The journal

    Nothing leaves yet. The implant keeps a quiet record in memory that survives sleep, waiting for a window when nobody is watching the traffic.

  4. 04

    The beacon

    A few kilobytes ride out inside a packet dressed as routine pairing, or sit and wait for a network the device is already trusted on.

From the other side

A keyboard in disguise

To the host, the receiver is already a keyboard. Anything that can type can open a shell — no exploit, no malware file, no alarm.

A remote control

The same radio that beacons out can carry instructions back in: pull the buffer, arm the microphone, hold a key down.

A signature of you

Movement timing is more personal than a password. Hesitation, fatigue and working hours are all readable from a trajectory.

Described, not demonstrated.

Modes

Three jobs. None of them need a cloud.

Most people own a mouse, a clicker and a TV remote that never sits where it should. Pivot One folds all three into one object you already pick up first — and every one of those jobs runs on the device, not on a service.

Pointer

A 26,000 DPI sensor and 1,000 Hz polling. It is a serious mouse well before it is anything else.

Presenter

Laser dot, next and back, instant blackout. Thirty metres of range from the back of the room.

Media

Volume wheel, play and pause, input switching — the TV remote that was always under your hand.

The pivot

Turn the shell ninety degrees. Mechanical detents find the click and the wheel becomes a d-pad — only a hand can start it.

Detection · on the wire

What Wireshark sees.

The implant can hide its firmware, its journal and its radio. It cannot hide its packets. A real mouse is silent on the network; once the receiver has typed a loader into the host, the beacon leaves over the uplink the machine already trusts — ordinary egress, which is exactly where a capture catches it. The payload is encrypted. The destination, the cadence and the volume are not.

01

A peripheral on the network

A mouse receiver has no business making DNS queries or opening sockets. The first packet from it is already the whole story.

02

Beacons on a clock

Fixed-size records leaving every 40–50 s, including while nobody is at the desk. Human traffic is bursty and irregular; an implant keeps time.

03

Volume without activity

Tens of kilobytes outbound to one unknown host during an idle, locked session. You cannot read it — you do not need to.

Simulated capture. No live traffic. The host, relay and addresses are RFC 5737 documentation examples.

The evidence

What it would take. What was removed.

Surveillance hardware is not clever. It is a short list of capabilities, each of which has to be designed in on purpose. Here is the list, beside the device that does without it.

A hinge is not remote-triggerable.

The only way Pivot One changes job is a hand on the shell. That is the whole security model: single-purpose hardware with no second owner, no service behind it and nothing to negotiate with.
